Roddy

An offline, full-duplex voice agent for Indian languages that listens and speaks at the same time, thinks with a strong language model, and runs fully offline on a 2019 budget laptop: a 2-core Intel i3 with no graphics card. Trained entirely on free Kaggle GPUs.

Plan v7 · 19 Nov 2026Course: LightningLM cohort (120B MoE final project)Status: proposal, nothing built

1. Summary

Every major lab is building voice agents. None of the open ones does all four of the things India needs at once: Indian languages and code-mixing, natural full-duplex conversation (listening while speaking, backchannels, interruptions), real intelligence (not just fluent noise), and running fully offline on the cheap laptops most people actually own. Roddy is a project to build that system and publish everything needed for others to reproduce it.

Roddy splits the job between two models. A small talker (about 0.6B parameters, small enough to train on free Kaggle T4 GPUs) owns the conversation's timing: it hears the user every 80 ms, decides when to speak, says "haan" or "achha" while the user talks, and stops when interrupted. A larger thinker (a per-language slice of a mixture-of-experts LLM, including your course's 120B) decides what to say and streams that content into the talker. Both run together on one Intel i3-10110U laptop (2 cores, 15 W, 24 GB RAM, integrated graphics only) with no internet connection.

2. The gap, with evidence

  • Human-1 (Josh Talks, April 2026) is the first open full-duplex model for any Indian language. It adapts Moshi to Hindi with 26,000 hours of real two-person conversations on 8×H100. It sounds natural (4.10/5) but only about 53% of replies follow the prompt and about 42% are complete. It is Hindi only and has no on-device result. arXiv:2604.23295
  • Sarvam Edge (February 2026) runs speech recognition (74M), TTS (24M) and translation (150M) on phones, but has no on-device LLM and no speech-to-speech model. Sarvam blog
  • SiMa.ai Palette 3.0 (17 November 2026) added mixture-of-experts LLMs (GPT-OSS-20B confirmed on the 16 GB module), EAGLE3/DFlash speculative decoding (up to 3.5× tokens/s), speech recognition over PCIe, and several models running at once. Edge-chip makers are building for exactly this kind of workload, but no Indian-language voice agent has been shown on any edge accelerator. Roddy does not need a Modalix: it targets a 2-core laptop with about a fifth of Modalix's memory bandwidth, so whatever runs there has headroom on an edge chip, and it reports results so a chip maker can judge the fit (section 6.4). SiMa press release
  • Full-duplex research is moving fast but is English- and Chinese-centred: 23 full-duplex models were released in Q4 2026 alone, per the fullduplex.ai tracker, and its latest weekly report lists no Indian-language or on-device duplex work. fullduplex.ai W47
  • Native duplex models are fluent but weak at reasoning. The D2 paper (October 2026) states that native full-duplex models "often lag behind strong half-duplex spoken language models in audio understanding, knowledge, reasoning, and instruction following." arXiv:2610.17124 Roddy's thinker–talker split is aimed directly at this.

2.1 Scoreboard against Sarvam

Sarvam is the strongest Indian-language voice company today, so it is the bar. Beating it on every axis is not reachable with one laptop and free GPUs: it covers 22 languages, Bulbul V4 is a large expressive voice model, and Sarvam 105B is a frontier-scale LLM. Roddy aims to beat it on the six axes that decide how a conversation feels, get close on two, and openly trail on two.

3. What is what: terms used in this plan

TermMeaning
Cascaded pipelineSpeech recognition → text LLM → text-to-speech, run one after another. Easy to build and debug, but each hop adds latency and it cannot listen while speaking.
Half-duplexThe system either listens or speaks, never both. Turn ends are found by silence detection or a turn-detector model.
Full-duplexThe model hears the user and produces its own audio continuously and simultaneously, so it can backchannel, be interrupted, and overlap like people do.
Neural audio codecA network that compresses audio into discrete tokens and back. Mimi (Kyutai) turns 24 kHz audio into 12.5 frames per second at 1.1 kbps, with 80 ms latency.
Frame rateHow many token steps per second of audio. Lower is cheaper for the language model: 12.5 Hz means one step every 80 ms.
RVQ codebooksResidual vector quantisation: each frame is several stacked tokens (Moshi uses 8), coarse first, fine detail later.
Temporal / depth transformerMoshi's split: a big temporal transformer steps once per frame; a small depth transformer predicts the 8 codebook tokens inside each frame.
Inner monologueThe model also emits the text of what it is saying, aligned to its audio. This greatly improves quality and is the hook where Roddy's thinker injects content.
Thinker–talkerQwen-Omni's design: a thinker LLM produces text and hidden states; a talker turns them into streaming speech. Roddy extends it so the talker also owns turn-taking.
BackchannelShort listener sounds ("haan", "hmm", "achha", "sari") that signal attention without taking the turn.
Barge-inThe user interrupts while the system is speaking; the system should stop quickly and listen.
TOR (takeover rate)Full-Duplex-Bench's measure of how often the model takes the turn in a given situation; low is good during pauses, high is good after a real turn end.
RTFReal-time factor: compute time ÷ audio duration. Must stay below 1 for live speech.
TTFATime to first audio: delay from the end of input to the first sound out.
MoE / expert slicingMixture of experts: only a few expert blocks run per token. Slicing keeps only the experts a language actually uses, so a big MoE fits a small device.
W4A16 / W8A8Weight and activation precision on the chip: 4-bit weights with 16-bit activations, and so on. SiMa's LLiMa uses int4 weights for most LLMs.
Speculative decodingA cheap draft proposes several tokens; the big model checks them in one pass. Palette 3.0 ships EAGLE3, DFlash and Gemma 4 MTP variants.
Memory bandwidthOn edge devices, token generation is limited by how fast weights stream from DRAM, not by TOPS. SiMa's Modalix and NVIDIA's Jetson Orin Nano Super have about 102 GB/s; this project's i3 laptop (dual-channel DDR4-2666) 43 GB/s on paper and ~21 GB/s measured.
Integrated GPU (iGPU)The graphics built into the CPU chip (here Intel UHD 620). It shares system RAM but adds a few hundred GFLOPS of fp16 compute, more than both CPU cores together. Intel's OpenVINO runtime can run models on it.
T4-hourOne hour on one NVIDIA T4 (16 GB, fp16, no bf16). One hour of a Kaggle "T4 ×2" session is two T4-hours. The compute unit in section 11.

How a turn flows

  1. Every 80 ms the talker reads one frame of user audio and one frame of its own previous output, and writes one frame of output (which may be silence).
  2. The talker also writes a running transcript of the user (an ASR-like text stream) and a turn-state signal: user speaking, user paused, user finished, user interrupting.
  3. The thinker receives the transcript continuously. It starts drafting while the user is still talking (speculative start) and commits when the talker signals a likely turn end.
  4. The thinker streams its reply as text tokens plus projected hidden states into the talker's inner-monologue slot. The talker speaks them with natural prosody and timing.
  5. If the thinker is late, the talker covers the gap with natural fillers ("haan, ek second…") rather than dead air; this is learned behaviour, not a hard-coded rule.
  6. If the user interrupts, the talker stops within a few frames and tells the thinker to discard the rest of the plan.

RAM size is not the limit: 24 GB holds a whole 30B MoE. Bandwidth is. That is exactly the case MoE is built for: a large model in total, a small part active per token. One catch: with a 16 GB and an 8 GB stick, only 16 GB runs dual-channel and the rest at half speed. Optional fix for ₹2–3k: replace the 8 GB stick with a 16 GB one.

6.3 Bandwidth and compute budget

At batch size 1, every decoding step reads the active weights from RAM once. Measured on this laptop: ~21 GB/s (a memory copy; 43 GB/s theoretical for dual-channel DDR4-2666). Plan to use ≤ 15 GB/s, about 70%.

The thinker only has to keep up with speech, not with a reader. People speak Hindi at roughly 2.5–3 words per second. With an Indic-efficient tokenizer (BrahmicTokenizer-131K, Sarvam's) that is about 4–6 tokens per second, to be measured in Stage 0. So the thinker's target is ~5 tokens/s, not the 20+ expected from chat models.

ComponentArithmeticGB/s
Talker temporal transformer (0.6B, int4)0.35 GB × 12.5 steps/s~4.4
Depth transformer (~50M, int4)0.025 GB × 8 codebooks × 12.5~2.5
Codec encode + decode~0.1 GB × 2 × 12.5~2.5
Thinker (2.4B active, ~3.5-bit)~1.05 GB × 5 tokens/s~5.3
Total≈ 15 GB/s (≈ 70% of measured)

Compute is the second limit. One 80 ms talker frame needs roughly 1.5–2 GFLOP (talker + depth + codec). The UHD 620 peaks at a few hundred GFLOPS in fp16, so that is about 10–20 ms if OpenVINO reaches a fair share of peak. The thinker needs ~5 GFLOP per token, ~25 GFLOP/s at 5 tokens/s, which the two AVX2 CPU cores can supply because the talker is not using them. Stage 0 measures both before anything is trained.

Consequence: a 7B Moshi-size talker would need ~44 GB/s at int4, twice this laptop's total bandwidth, and a dense 8B thinker at 5 tokens/s would need ~20 GB/s on its own. Hence a ~0.6B talker on the iGPU and an MoE thinker with ≤ 2.5B active parameters on the CPU. The course 120B (5.93B active, top-12 routing) is too heavy as it stands; Stage 5 tests slicing it by language and cutting routing to top-4 (roughly 2–3B active) with a short healing fine-tune.

6.4 Reporting that edge-chip makers can use

Roddy does not run on a Modalix or a Jetson. It runs on something weaker: a 2-core i3 with about a fifth of their memory bandwidth, splitting work between CPU and integrated GPU much as an edge chip splits it between CPU and accelerator. Every result is reported in units that carry over. To make that easy for SiMa.ai and other chip makers to see:

  • Report every component's needs in hardware-neutral units (bytes read per 80 ms frame, GB/s, operations per frame, peak memory) next to measured latency and watts.
  • Bandwidth sweep: run with 1, 2 and 4 CPU threads and with the iGPU on and off, measure the GB/s actually used, and plot latency against bandwidth, so a chip maker can read off where their chip would land.
  • Publish a small budget calculator: given a device's memory, bandwidth and power, it says which talker and thinker sizes fit.
  • Use only standard operators (transformer blocks, convolutions, int4/int8 weights) and export to ONNX so any vendor's compiler can try Roddy.
  • Show the real-time pattern edge runtimes are built for: a hard-deadline model (the talker, every 80 ms) sharing one device with a best-effort model (the thinker), with measured priority behaviour.
  • Show MoE expert slicing as the way to fit a big model on a small device, the same direction as Palette 3.0's MoE support.

8.3 The data factory

  1. Fetch: stream each source from the Hugging Face Hub or its official mirror, one shard at a time; never keep raw audio beyond the shard being processed.
  2. Clean: mono, resample to 24 kHz (Mimi's rate), normalise loudness, reject clipped files.
  3. Segment: voice-activity detection into 2–30 s clips; keep conversational sessions whole for timing statistics.
  4. Identify: language ID per clip; mark code-mixed clips from word-level script and language tags on the transcript.
  5. Label: keep human transcripts; pseudo-label the rest with Indic-Transcribe and IndicConformer and keep clips where they agree.
  6. Score: signal-to-noise ratio, agreement CER, speaking rate, speaker ID.
  7. Tokenise: Mimi, 8 codebooks at 12.5 Hz (~7 GB per 10,000 h).
  8. Shard: tokens + text + metadata into Parquet shards on a private Hugging Face dataset.
  9. Card: one data card per source: licence, hours per language, speakers, every filter and how many clips it removed.

Where it runs: code is written and tested on 10-minute samples on the laptop; steps 1–4 and 6 run in Kaggle CPU sessions (no weekly quota); steps 5 and 7 use Kaggle T4s.

9. Stages

Each stage has an exit gate. A stage that fails its gate drops to its fallback so the project always has something shippable. Week numbers assume a 24-week course; scale proportionally if yours is shorter.

Stage 0 · Foundations weeks 1–2 all

Goal
Lock scope, tooling and baselines so later stages are measured against facts.
Tasks
  • Monorepo with training, data, eval and edge folders; experiment tracking; data-card template.
  • Licence audit for every corpus and model in sections 4 and 8.
  • Laptop is known: i3-10110U, UHD 620, 24 GB DDR4-2666, ~21 GB/s measured copy. Set up OpenVINO (CPU + iGPU) and llama.cpp; run Sarvam 30B and a 1–2B dense model at int4 and record tokens/s; time one 80 ms frame of a dummy 0.6B transformer + Mimi on the iGPU.
  • Kaggle template notebook: checkpoint every 30 minutes, resume after the 12-hour cut-off, data and checkpoints on the Hugging Face Hub, runs as a background commit.
  • Build the evaluation harness skeleton: latency probes, ASR round-trip CER, Full-Duplex-Bench v1 runner.
  • Measure baselines on 50 Indic test clips: Moshi, MiniCPM-o 4.5, Human-1 (if weights are available), cascaded IndicConformer → LLM → IndicF5 (large ones on a Kaggle T4).
Deliverables
Repo, licence table, baseline numbers, confirmed targets for section 1.
Exit gate
A dummy talker frame runs in ≤ 40 ms on the iGPU while the thinker decodes ≥ 5 tokens/s on the CPU; a Kaggle job survives a forced restart; baseline table filled.
If it fails
Frame too slow: cut the depth transformer to 4–6 codebooks or the talker to 0.3B. Thinker too slow: use the 1–2B dense thinker. Both too slow: the i3 runs the half-duplex Stage 1 floor and full Roddy is shown on a T4.

Stage 1 · Cascaded floor on the laptop weeks 2–5 guaranteed result

Goal
An offline Indian-language voice assistant on the laptop using a cascaded pipeline. This alone already goes beyond Sarvam Edge, which has no on-device LLM.
Tasks
  • Streaming VAD + semantic turn detector (text-based end-of-turn classifier on partial transcripts).
  • IndicConformer (or Sarvam Edge-class) streaming ASR.
  • Thinker v0: a 1–2B Indic-capable LLM at int4 (Sarvam 30B if Stage 0 shows it keeps up), prompted for spoken style.
  • Streaming TTS: DhVaani (~0.1B; Indic Parler at ~0.9B is too slow for the i3), sentence-chunked.
  • Barge-in: stop TTS on detected user speech.
  • Measure end-to-end latency, power and memory.
Deliverables
Demo video; latency/power report; the baseline every later stage must beat.
Exit gate
≤ 1.5 s p50 response latency on the i3, 5 languages working.
If it fails
Reduce to Hindi + Hinglish; use a smaller thinker.

Stage 2 · Speech tokenizer weeks 3–6 speech

Goal
Pick and, if needed, adapt the codec for Indian languages.
Tasks
  • Evaluate Mimi and NVIDIA's 105-language codec per language: PESQ, STOI, speaker similarity, and ASR round-trip CER on IndicVoices-R test audio.
  • Specific checks: retroflex vs dental consonants, aspiration, gemination, vowel length.
  • No codec training by default. Only if one language is clearly worse, fine-tune just the Mimi decoder on IndicVoices-R, capped at ~30 T4-hours.
  • Tokenise all training audio to Mimi tokens on Kaggle once; measure throughput in week 3 and store shards on the Hugging Face Hub.
  • Check that the codec runs within budget on the i3's iGPU.
Deliverables
Codec decision memo with per-language numbers; fine-tuned weights if needed.
Exit gate
ASR round-trip CER on resynthesised audio within 2 points of the original for every language.
If it fails
Use the better codec per language family, or accept a higher bitrate (more codebooks).

Stage 3 · Data factory weeks 3–10 largest risk

Goal
Run data phases D0–D7 (section 8.5) and pass their gates.
Tasks
  1. Assemble and clean the audio-text pretraining set; pseudo-label raw audio with the best available ASR; filter.
  2. Measure real Indic conversation statistics (gaps, overlaps, backchannel rates and forms per language) from Nirantar and IndicVoices conversational audio.
  3. Build and validate the Indic mono-to-stereo splitter; report its error rate on a hand-checked set.
  4. Build the synthetic dialogue generator using those statistics; generate in batches with diversity checks.
  5. Human spot-check: 2 native speakers per language rate 100 random clips for naturalness.
  6. Freeze train/validation/test splits with speaker-disjoint test sets.
Deliverables
Datasets with data cards; conversation-statistics report; splitter tool.
Exit gate
≥ 5k h pretraining audio (as tokens); ≥ 1k h two-channel dialogue; spot-check naturalness ≥ 3.5/5.
If it fails
Cut to 3 languages; increase synthetic share; buy or record a small real Hindi conversation set for fine-tuning.

Stage 4 · Talker weeks 6–12 speech

Goal
A ~0.6B duplex talker that hears and speaks Indian languages with natural timing, trained entirely on Kaggle T4s.
Tasks
  1. 4a Audio-text pretraining: initialise the temporal transformer from a ~0.6B open LM; add Mimi-token embeddings and a ~100M depth transformer; train on interleaved audio and text with an inner-monologue text stream. fp16 mixed precision (T4 has no bf16), DDP across the two T4s, gradient checkpointing, checkpoint every 30 minutes.
  2. 4b Duplex fine-tuning: train on two-channel data (split real + synthetic) with separate user and assistant streams, as in Moshi.
  3. 4c Behaviour tuning: preference or RL tuning for pause handling, backchannel timing and interruption response, following PersonaPlex's RL post-training on Seamless Interaction.
  4. Ablate talker size (0.3B / 0.6B) against the bandwidth budget; 1B only with LoRA or course-cluster time.
  5. Budget: ~150–250 T4-hours for 4a–4c (section 11.2).
Deliverables
Talker checkpoints; Full-Duplex-Bench-style timing numbers on Indic test clips.
Exit gate
Turn-taking and interruption takeover rates ≥ 0.9 on held-out Indic clips; speech CER ≤ 15%.
If it fails
Use the talker only as a turn-taking and backchannel controller over the Stage 1 TTS (a "semi-duplex" system).

Stage 5 · Thinker weeks 4–12, in parallel LLM · course link

Goal
A strong Indian-language reasoning model that fits in ~14 GB of RAM and decodes ≥ 5 tokens/s (speaking speed) on the i3's two CPU cores while the talker runs.
Tasks
  1. Baseline: a 1–2B Indic-capable dense model at int4; measure Indic quality.
  2. Sarvam 30B (128 experts, 2.4B active): log expert routing on text from each target language; prune rarely used experts; measure quality loss (on a Kaggle 2× T4 with the model loaded in 4-bit).
  3. Course 120B: add language-aware routing during the course run so experts specialise by language family; extract per-language slices (MeLt-style). This is the research link between Roddy and the course project. Then cut routing from top-12 to top-4 and heal with a short QLoRA fine-tune on Kaggle, so active parameters fall from ~5.9B to roughly 2–3B. The open question: how much quality survives.
  4. Spoken-style SFT with QLoRA on Kaggle: short replies, spoken numbers, code-mixing on demand, tool-call format.
  5. Incremental prefill: feed the user's transcript into the thinker as it arrives, so the i3's slow prompt processing stays off the critical path.
  6. Speculative decoding draft head (EAGLE3-style) for the chosen thinker.
Deliverables
Thinker slices with size/quality table per language; draft heads.
Exit gate
Fits ≤ 14 GB; ≥ 5 tokens/s on the i3 alongside the talker; Indic QA within 10% of the unpruned model.
If it fails
Ship with the dense baseline; keep the slicing work as a separate paper.

Stage 6 · Coupling thinker and talker weeks 12–16 core novelty

Goal
Make the talker speak the thinker's content naturally, in real time, with interruptions and fillers.
Tasks
  1. Projection adapters (~100M) from thinker hidden states into the talker's inner-monologue slot; thinker frozen in 4-bit on one T4, talker on the other, as in the 97M-adapter recipe (~100–200 T4-hours).
  2. Asynchronous, time-sliced runtime: talker at 12.5 Hz, thinker streaming on its own clock (DuplexOmni pattern).
  3. Speculative start: thinker drafts while the user speaks; commit or discard on the talker's turn-state signal.
  4. Gap handling: train the talker to produce natural fillers when the plan stream is late.
  5. Interruption protocol: talker stops, thinker receives the cut-off point and the new user speech.
  6. Tool calls: timers, device controls and local retrieval, tested with Full-Duplex-Bench v3-style scenarios.
Deliverables
Coupled model running on a T4 and the laptop GPU; ablation of text-only vs text + hidden-state coupling.
Exit gate
On a GPU: ≥ 80% complete replies, ≥ 75% prompt-following, p50 latency ≤ 400 ms.
If it fails
Text-only coupling (thinker text → talker inner monologue), which is simpler and still duplex.

Stage 7 · Laptop port and edge-class optimisation weeks 14–20 systems

Goal
Roddy running offline on the i3 laptop, within the budgets of section 6.
Tasks
  1. Export talker, codec and thinker using standard operators (OpenVINO for the iGPU; llama.cpp or OpenVINO for the CPU); int4 weights, 16-bit or 8-bit activations; check accuracy after each quantisation step.
  2. Scheduler: talker and codec on the iGPU as the hard-deadline lane; thinker on the CPU cores, best-effort, with speculative decoding; audio I/O at high priority.
  3. Memory map and KV-cache limits; sliding-window talker context.
  4. Bandwidth sweep (1, 2, 4 threads; iGPU on and off): latency against measured GB/s, as in section 6.4.
  5. Power and thermal soak: 30-minute continuous conversation at room and high ambient temperature.
  6. Robustness: far-field mic, noise, low-volume speakers.
Deliverables
On-device build; latency, power and memory report; demo videos per language.
Exit gate
p50 ≤ 800 ms on the i3, fits in 24 GB RAM, software-measured power reported, no dropped frames in a 30-minute soak.
If it fails
Shrink the talker or its codebooks; use the 1–2B thinker; or ship the i3 half-duplex floor plus full Roddy on a T4.

Stage 8 · Indic-FDB benchmark weeks 8–22 evaluation

Goal
The first open full-duplex benchmark for Indian languages.
Tasks
  1. Port Full-Duplex-Bench v1 dimensions (pause handling, backchannels, smooth turn-taking, interruptions) to 5 languages + Hinglish, following the French FDB precedent.
  2. Add Indic-specific dimensions: code-switching mid-turn, culturally typical backchannels, honorific/register consistency, spoken numbers and Indian names.
  3. Record real test conversations with consenting native speakers; noisy-environment variants.
  4. Automated examiner (FDB v2 style) for multi-turn quality; human MOS protocol with native raters.
  5. Run Roddy, Human-1, Moshi, MiniCPM-o and the cascaded floor.
Deliverables
Benchmark release (audio, scripts, leaderboard table, paper section).
Exit gate
≥ 300 scored items per language; inter-rater agreement reported.
If it fails
Release a smaller Hindi + Hinglish + Tamil version.

Stage 9 · Release and outreach weeks 20–24 all

Goal
Make the work usable and visible.
Tasks
  • Release weights, data recipes, splitter, Indic-FDB and the laptop build scripts and Kaggle notebooks with licences and model cards.
  • Paper: architecture, data recipe, edge results, benchmark.
  • Demo videos: one per language, real devices, unedited latency.
  • Outreach: share the hardware-neutral budget report with edge-chip makers (SiMa.ai developer forum, NVIDIA Jetson community) and offer Roddy as a reference workload; Sarvam (fills the on-device LLM and speech-to-speech gap in Sarvam Edge); course showcase.
Deliverables
Public release, paper draft, demo reel.
Exit gate
A stranger can reproduce the Stage 1 floor and run Roddy from the README.

13. Speed playbook

  1. Bandwidth first. On the i3 (~21 GB/s) and on 100 GB/s-class edge chips (Modalix, Jetson, phones), tokens per second are bounded by bytes streamed per step. Keep the talker ≤ 0.6B and the thinker's active parameters ≤ 2.5B, both at about 4 bits.
  2. Speaking-speed thinker. The thinker needs only ~5 tokens/s to keep up with speech; spend the rest of the budget on the talker's frame deadline.
  3. Use the iGPU. Put the talker and codec on the UHD 620 so the hard-deadline loop never competes with the thinker for CPU cores.
  4. Speculative start. Let the thinker draft during the user's turn; most of its latency then disappears from the critical path.
  5. Speculative decoding for the thinker with an EAGLE3-style draft head (SiMa reports up to 3.5× tokens/s from EAGLE3/DFlash on its chip).
  6. Priorities. The talker's 80 ms loop must never wait on the thinker. Run them as separate processes or streams with the talker at higher priority, and keep the codec on its own lane.
  7. Short depth transformer. Predict fewer codebooks for early frames (coarse first) if the frame deadline is at risk.
  8. Sliding contexts. Cap the talker KV cache (a few minutes at 12.5 Hz); summarise older dialogue into the thinker's context.
  9. Lower frame rate experiments. Try a 6.25 Hz codec (FlexiCodec) to halve talker steps if quality holds.
  10. Measure p95, not just p50. Long-tail stalls are what users notice.

14. Quality playbook

  1. Intelligence lives in the thinker. Do not try to make the talker smart; make it a good listener and a good speaker.
  2. Real statistics for synthetic data. Overlap, gap and backchannel distributions must come from real Indic conversations.
  3. Native-speaker review at every stage gate, per language, with a written rubric.
  4. Spoken-style thinker. Short sentences, spoken numbers and dates, Indian names pronounced correctly, no lists or markdown.
  5. Code-mixing by design. Include Hinglish (and Tanglish where data allows) in every data stream, written in each language's own script.
  6. Behaviour tuning after SFT for pause handling and backchannels, as PersonaPlex did.
  7. Safety. No voice cloning of real people without consent; refusal behaviour in all languages; on-device by default for privacy.
